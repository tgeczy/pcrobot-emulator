PC-ROBOT (MULTIVOX) for NVDA

This add-on speaks with PC-ROBOT / RobotVOX, the Hungarian speech synthesizer sold by Nikol Elektronika of Budapest in 1994. It is the software descendant of MULTIVOX, the formant synthesizer developed at the Hungarian Academy of Sciences' phonetics laboratory, which originally shipped as an ISA expansion card.

How it works

Nothing about the voice is re-implemented. The original DOS programs (SBDD.EXE, the Sound Blaster driver, and ROBOTVOX.EXE, the speech engine) are run in DOSBox-X with the mixer muted, so nothing is heard on the host. DOSBox records the audio the sound card would have played, and the add-on hands that PCM to NVDA. A small resident DOS program, SPEAK.COM, passes text to the engine through its INT 2Fh service.

Requirements

DOSBox-X must be installed. The add-on looks for it in C:\DOSBox-X and in Program Files.

Settings

  • Voice — the engine's two male voices (Férfi 1 and Férfi 2).
  • Rate — mapped to the engine's speed setting (1 fastest to 7 normal).
  • Pitch — mapped to 55–155 Hz, so NVDA's default sits on the engine's own resting voice of about 105 Hz.
  • Volume — the engine's own 0–13 amplitude.
  • Articulation — 0 (continuous) to 5 (very segmented speech).
  • Whisper — turns the engine's whisper mode on.

First selection: about ten seconds of silence, with beeps

When you choose PC-ROBOT, a whole DOS machine has to boot and the engine speaks its own announcement ("Multimédia robot, start") inside the muted machine, which the add-on waits out so it does not contaminate your first sentence. That takes roughly ten seconds, and NVDA has no voice at all during it.

So the add-on beeps: a low beep when it starts booting and a higher beep when the robot is ready to talk. A long low beep means the engine failed to start — check the NVDA log.

After that, speech is streamed as the robot produces it, so each utterance starts promptly. Anything NVDA said while the engine was booting is spoken once it is ready.

The speech engine and voice data are © Nikol Elektronika, Budapest. They are included here to keep a piece of Hungarian speech-technology history usable.

The DOS window

DOSBox is hidden as soon as it starts and the keyboard is handed straight back to whatever you were using, so it should never take focus or be announced.

Latency

Audio has to travel from the DOS engine through DOSBox's recorder before NVDA can play it, so expect roughly half a second before speech starts. Once the engine is warm an utterance costs little more than its own length, and interrupting (arrowing quickly through a menu, for example) silences the engine straight away.
